Linking words

According to official Coherence and Cohesion band descriptors, securing a high band score requires a flexible and accurate range of cohesive devices. Master these functional categories below.

1. Addition & Progression

Use these to expand your points logically without relying exclusively on "and".

  • Furthermore / Moreover: Formal, adds a stronger or more important point.
    Example: The new architecture of the museum is stunning; furthermore, it is completely energy-efficient.
  • Additionally / In addition to this: Standard formal addition.
  • Not only... but also: A correlative conjunction that adds surprising or impressive information.
  • Likewise / Similarly: Used to show that two things are alike.
    Example: The vocabulary module improves writing; similarly, it enhances speaking fluency.

2. Result & Consequence

Essential for showing cause and effect in Task 2 essays or speaking explanations.

  • Consequently / As a result: Formal linking adverbs (often starting a new sentence or following a semicolon).
  • Therefore / Thus: Indicates a logical conclusion based on previous evidence.
  • Hence: Formal, meaning "for this reason".
    Example: The construction site management delayed the delivery of materials; hence, the project was pushed back by a week.

3. Giving Examples & Clarification

  • For instance / For example: Used to introduce a specific scenario.
  • Namely: Used to specifically identify what you are referring to.
    Example: The curriculum focuses on three core subjects, namely math, science, and literacy.
  • To illustrate: An excellent formal phrase for essays before providing a detailed example.

4. Sequencing & Time

Crucial for describing processes, phases, or recounting a series of events smoothly.

  • Subsequently: Happening after something else.
    Example: The architects finalized the blueprint; subsequently, the excavation crew broke ground.
  • Meanwhile / In the meantime: Happening at the same time as another event.
    Example: The mountain biking participants prepared for the Campground Challenge; meanwhile, the mechanics performed safety checks on the gear.

5. Condition

Used to state that one thing depends on another thing happening.

  • Provided that / Providing that: A more formal way of saying "if".
    Example: The alpine mountain biking event will proceed provided that the trail conditions remain safe.
  • Unless: Means "if not" or "except if".
  • Alternatively: Used to introduce a second possibility or choice.

6. The Concession Group (Contrast)

It is crucial to know the grammatical difference between words that express contrast.

  • Although, Even though, Though: Followed by a full clause (Subject + Verb).
    Example: Even though AI is taking over many jobs, new industries are emerging.
  • Despite, In spite of: Followed by a Noun, Pronoun, or a Gerund (Verb+ing).
    Example: Despite the high inflation, consumers keep buying.
  • Nevertheless / Nonetheless: Used to say something is true in spite of what was just said.

7. Emphasis & Summary

Perfect for concluding paragraphs or emphasizing key facts in evaluations.

  • Indeed / Undoubtedly: Used to emphasize a statement or confirm something is true.
  • Primarily: For the most part; mainly.
  • To sum up / Ultimately: Excellent for concluding a complex evaluation or an IELTS task 2 essay.

Advanced Grammatical Structures

Master these core syntactic structures to demonstrate a higher level of language proficiency and precision in both spoken and written contexts.

1. Conditionals (Mixed & Advanced)

Conditionals express the result of a certain condition. Advanced learners must master Third and Mixed Conditionals.

  • Zero/First/Second: General truths (Zero), future possibilities (First), and present/future hypotheticals (Second).
  • Third Conditional: Past hypotheticals. If + Past Perfect, would have + Past Participle.
    (e.g., If I had studied, I would have passed.)
  • Mixed Conditionals: Mixing time frames. Past action with a present result. If + Past Perfect, would + bare infinitive.
    (e.g., If I had won the lottery yesterday, I would be rich today.)

2. The Passive Voice

Used to shift focus from the "doer" of an action to the action itself or the receiver of the action.

  • Structure: Object + verb "to be" (in the correct tense) + Past Participle.
  • Impersonal Passives: Common in formal reports. (e.g., It is believed that... or The data is said to be...)

Practice (12 Questions): Conjugate the verb in parenthesis into the correct passive form.

3. Advanced Uncountable Nouns

Uncountable nouns cannot be counted directly and do not take a plural 's'. Advanced English features tricky uncountable concepts.

  • Common Traps: advice, equipment, furniture, luggage, research, evidence, information, knowledge, news, progress, homework, machinery.
  • Rule: They take a singular verb and cannot follow "a/an" or a number directly (use "a piece of" instead).

Practice (12 Questions): Type the correct form of the noun.

4. Irregular Advanced Plurals

Many academic and scientific words derived from Latin or Greek have highly irregular plural forms.

  • -um to -a: datum → data, bacterium → bacteria
  • -on to -a: criterion → criteria, phenomenon → phenomena
  • -is to -es: analysis → analyses, hypothesis → hypotheses, crisis → crises
  • -us to -i: stimulus → stimuli, fungus → fungi, syllabus → syllabi

5. Advanced Comparison Structures

Go beyond simple comparatives to show nuanced relationships between variables.

  • Double Comparatives: The + comparative..., the + comparative... (e.g., The harder you study, the better you perform.)
  • Modifiers: Use adverbs to emphasize difference (e.g., significantly better, nowhere near as difficult, by far the most, slightly less).

Writing a Report to Describe a Graph

Describing Trends: Adjectives, Adverbs & Verbs

When describing lines, bar charts, or trends over time, you must vary your sentence structures to achieve a high band score.

  • VERBS: Show the action or change.
    Examples: increased, rose, climbed, decreased, fell, dropped, fluctuated, plateaued.
  • ADVERBS: Describe how the action happened (usually ending in -ly). They pair with Verbs.
    - Formula 1: Subject + Verb + Adverb (e.g., Sales fell dramatically.)
  • ADJECTIVES: Describe the noun (the change itself). They pair with Nouns.
    - Formula 2: There was a/an + Adjective + Noun + in... (e.g., There was a dramatic fall in sales.)
